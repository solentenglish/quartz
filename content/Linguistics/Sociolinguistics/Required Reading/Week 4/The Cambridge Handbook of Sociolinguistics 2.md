### Introduction

There has been a transformation in the way that gender is viewed from a binary approach to a more complex non-binary one.

> ... the focus shift to viewing gender not as an attribute but as an interactional achievement, as a performance rather than a “given,” as an enactment that can manifest itself differently in different cultures, communities, sub-communities, and even individuals, whether over the course of the life span or across and within everyday interactions.
### Approaches to Language and Gender Studies

There has traditionally been a tripartite view division of studies into gender, namely the 'deficit', 'dominance', and 'difference' approaches. There is perhaps some overlap between the first two. As more nuanced view of gender is being introduced, perhaps it makes more sense to talk of a dichotomy between difference and diversity.

A variety of approaches has also been used, largely from the Labovian and pramagatic perspectives.
### Early Approaches: "Female Deficit"
#### "Women's language": Lexical and pragmatic features

Lakoff stated the following as features of female language:

- Use of precise colour terms
	- *mauve, magenta*
- Use of “empty” adjectives
	- *divine, cute*
- Use of high-rising intonation on statements
	- *My name is Mary Smith?*
- Frequent use of tag questions
	- *It’s hot in here, isn’t it?*
- Use of hypercorrect grammar
	- *going instead of goin’*
- Avoids non-standard grammar
	- *avoids ain’t*

Lakoff stated that this was a starting point rather than a careful study. She was working in more of a pre-Labovian tradition in which self reflection was key. Her findings were both contradicted and supported in subsequent studies. Hightlighting the importance of context.
#### Gender-based patterns

Work done in the variationist tradition highlighted differences in male and female speech. Labov created a set of rules based on his and others findings. The key ones were:

Principle 2: The Linguistic Conformity of Women: For stable sociolinguistic variables, women show a lower rate of stigmatized variants and a higher rate of prestige variants than men
Principle 3: In linguistic change from above women adopt prestige forms at a higher rate than men
Principle 4: In linguistic change from below, women use higher frequencies of innovative forms than men do

Labov's assertion that women have a  greater degree of concern for prestige forms places his work in the dominance / deficit tradition. Women are compensating for their relative lack of power. However, this does not explain why women innovate more than men overall.
### Difference-based Approaches

The following is a summary of Tannen's views on male and female language:

 > Girls grow up in groups in which the emphasis is on equality, cooperation, and friendships, and so they develop conversational styles that are cooperative and highly interactional ... . In addition, because close friendships are very important for girls, their talk is focused more on feelings than facts, and they become very attuned to the social and emotional messages behind the literal content of talk, or “metamessages.”
 > 
 > Boys, on the other hand, grow up in groups based on competition and hierarchy, and so they develop styles that are competitive rather than cooperative, often dominating conversations through long turns, interruptions, and abrupt introduction of new topics.

There is also a strong focus on the role of misunderstanding between men and women, e.g. whether back-channelling should be understood as encouragement (women) or agreement (men).

The approach has been criticised as downplaying the power differential between men and women.
### Dominance-based Approaches

A dominance-based approach differs from a difference based one in that misunderstandings are seen as being rooted in the patriarchal structure of society, indeed if they are misunderstandings at all. Men are able to leverage their position in society to control conversations and perpetuate pre-existing power differences.

A dominance-based approach can explain greater variation in female language than in male language.

>  Eckert suggests that girls (and women) make greater use of linguistic resources than males since females must rely on symbolic expressions of worth and symbolic means of attaining power (i.e. “symbolic capital”), since they are denied real worth in the linguistic marketplace. 
### Gender Diversity, Gender Performance
#### Localised communities, localised expectations

Studies have found that there is not always a clear cut divide between men and women. Other factors such as outside networks can play a significant role. When there are generational difference in working patterns within a gender then this can greatly affect the language used by a particular group, e.g. if younger women have service sector jobs which require them to speak standard forms.
 
 >  However, the gender-based patterning of variation across the generations is not straightforward and suggests interesting interrelations between language, gender, and globalization.

#### Communities of practices

Communities of practice are defined as:

> an aggregate of people who come together around some enterprise” and that is “simultaneously defined by its membership and the shared practice in which that membership engages.

They are different from a more traditional 'language community' in that they afford more agency to the participant who is a member of multiple overlapping communities, and together with other members, help to define the characteristics of a particular community.
#### Beyond mainstream gender identities

Research increasingly focuses on:

> researchers have moved from attempting to identify which features correlate with the speech of those who self-identify as, for example, gay or lesbian, to investigating how individuals and interactants use features and varieties that index various stances and social identities (or facets of identity) to shape and display multi- faceted and dynamic gendered identities. 
#### Problematizing the mainstream

> Finally, studies focusing on gender as performance demonstrate not only that all identities are gendered identities, even the most “unmarked,” but also that all gender performances/identities are colored by heteronormativity, including not only those that conform quite closely to established gender/sexuality norms but also highly nonconformist, “queer,” and marginalized identities. 
### The Study Continues

Future research will focus on fluidity and diversity but also the bounds in which these operate.
