### Introduction

There has long been interest in men's and women's history. More recent science-based approaches have largely been from feminists perspective.
### Women’s and Men’s Languages

The language itself sometimes makes a distinction between men's and women's language.

> Linguists studying several languages have found evidence of ‘sex-
exclusive’ language forms, that is, cases in which an obligatory grammatical distinction is made between female and male speakers.

When looking at real-world use, the picture is complicated by the confounding factors such as work such as age.

> Hlonipha refers to the avoidance, by married women, of any sylla-
bles that occur in the names of their in-laws – particularly the father-in-law,
mother-in-law, father-in-law’s brothers and their wives and father-in-law’s
sisters. 

The main points covered in this unit are:

- Gendered language may be a direct result of gender but may also be the result of gender adjacent issues such as status.
- Researchers have varying taken a 'difference' or a 'dominance' approach.
- Gendered language can be seen as both reflecting and maintaining gender differences.
- Terms such as ‘women’s language’ and ‘men’s language’ imply homogeneity
among women and men

### Variationist Studies: Quantifying Gender

Labov's work (which predominantly focused on social class) has been critiqued for paying too little attention to gender.

Lifestyle patterns have an impact upon the language that people use. Research has found that:

>in order to understand gender differences in language it is important to look at women’s and men’s lifestyles in different communities: whom they interact with, and what might motivate them to adopt certain varieties
### Gender in Interaction: ‘Deficit’, ‘Dominance’ and ‘Difference’

The study of gender differences in language has practical implications, particularly for education. There has been a large amount of research in the following areas:

- Amount of talk: male speakers talk more
- Interruptions: male speakers interrupt female speakers more
- Conversational support: female speakers more frequently provide support
- Tentativeness: female speakers hedge more
- Compliments: women both give and receive more compliments

##### **Difference Model**

**Key idea:** Men and women belong to different _subcultures_ and therefore use language differently.

- Associated with: **Deborah Tannen**
    
- Focus: **Miscommunication** due to different conversational styles rather than inequality.
    
- Example: Women might use language to **build relationships**, while men use it to **assert status**.
    
- Not about power — it’s about **different norms**.


---

##### **Deficit Model**

**Key idea:** Women’s language is seen as **lacking** or **inferior** compared to men’s.

- Associated with: **Robin Lakoff**
    
- Focus: Women's language shows **powerlessness** (e.g., hedges like _"sort of"_, tag questions like _"isn’t it?"_).
    
- Criticised for being outdated and **biased towards male norms**.
    

---

##### **Dominance Model**

**Key idea:** Language reflects and reinforces **male dominance** in society.

- Associated with: **Zimmerman & West**, **Dale Spender**
    
- Focus: Men use language to **control conversations** (e.g., interruptions, topic control).
    
- Language is a tool of **patriarchy**.

All three approaches have drawn criticisms. Lakoff's has been seen as male-orientated in that it presents male language as the norm against which female speak is compared. Tannen's work has been seen as giving men a free pass and ignoring the ways in which women are disadvantaged by gendered language.

> Feminism is not about celebrating the skills required of women by our present arrangements, but about changing those arrangements root and branch. Feminism must question sexual divisions of labour in every sphere of life

Critiques of the dominance model point to the fact that it paints women's language as being powerless when faced with male speech.

### Gender and Politeness

Politeness has been a focus of research on gender. Initial studies focused on how women's position in society (relative powerlessness) would likely make them value polite speech.

Question tags have been controversial. They have been used of a sign of women's tentativeness when offering opinions. 

Another interpretation is that they have more than one function and are partly a way of facilitating conversation.

> Holmes argues that facilitative tags express positive politeness, showing
concern for the listener. Women’s greater use of these suggested they
were more positively polite than men.

(Positive politeness can be contrasted with negative politeness which consists of avoiding topics which may cause your partner to lose face).

The research into question tags illustrates a central problem when analysing male and female speech, namely that it is difficult to assign a function to a particular speech act. There is wide room for interpretation when considering the function of question tags and other pertinent features of gendered speech.

### Contextualised Approaches: Performance and Performativity

Since the 1990s there has been:
- greater focus on the context of speech act when considering functions or meanings;
- a move to considering gender fluid and variable; -
- a preoccupation with the interactional ‘performance’ of gender.

The performative attitude can be summarised as:

> Whereas sociolinguistics traditionally assumes that people talk the way they do because of who they (already) are, the postmodernist approach suggests that people are who they
are because of (among other things) the way they talk.

Research has been done into the way individuals can pick up and put down gendered and sexually-orientated language depending on the situation and the image they which to portray.

There is debate about the practicality of highly localised studies.
### Conclusion

Sociolinguists often maintain the facade of neutrality with regard to different language varieties. This is not always the case when considering gender:

>  Some researchers have explicitly rejected impartiality and choose to ‘declare their interests’ rather than hide these behind a screen of objectivity.
