### Introduction

The construction of race identity is complex. Language plays a key role to the exist that people can be re-raced on the basis of their language proficiency.

> “the ideological link between language and ethnicity is so potent that the use of linguistic practices associated with a given ethnic group may be sufficient for an individual to pass as a group member
### Key Issues in the Discussion of Race and Ethnicity

Both race and ethnicity are socially constructed. They are also multi-faceted. Gender cannot be separated from ethnicity.

Self perception and the perception of others need to be taken into consideration.

> With respect to race and ethnicity, specifically, phenotype, language, and a number of other factors may affect how individuals are categorized by the dominant ideology of their communities. 
### Language Resources in Ethnic Identity

There are a number of ways that someone might index their ethnicity via language
- heritage language
- dialectical features
- suprasegmental features
- discourse features
- code-switching
- crossing

Speaking another language may also not signify membership of a particular group. It may be purely for practical reasons. An inability to speak a heritage language may put membership of an ethnic group at risk.

> Because the use of a standard variety is linked, practically and ideologically, with social class, individuals who belong to higher socio economic strata within minority ethnic groups may experience a conflict in terms of the linguistic pressures from the communities in which they interact.

Members of a community who move away and start speaking more prestigious forms can be criticised as selling out. When this happens, there is an interaction between class and ethnicity.

> Africans must balance conflicting pressures in the use of English along the acrolect-mesolect-basilect continuum that characterizes their varieties of English.
### The Interaction of Ethnicity with Other Social Factors

Initial studies focused on dialects focused on working class varieties because they were seen at the most 'different'. Middle class language has more recently come into view. Some middle class black Americans are comfortable code-switching from a young age. For others it is a more conscious decision.
### Social Class

Studies have found that people retain some features of AAVE even when speaking for a mixed audience. They are more likely to drop grammatical feature of AAVE than phonological ones.

> The contexts in which different varieties may be found cannot be separated neatly into those where a vernacular is “appropriate,” and those where it is not.
### Gender

Gender is increasingly seen as a constructed identity rather than an essentialist one.

> There are numerous studies showing that gender has a powerful role in affecting the use of linguistic features associated with a minority ethnic identity.

This is in part due to the different activities that individuals participate in.
### Age

Younger people more often view it as their right to speak AAVE. Crossing is associated with adolescents as it's seen as rebellious.
### Networks and Related Factors

Language change away from ethnic varieties can be predicted by prolonged contact with standard forms. This is not always the case however as:

> ethnicity can be a very strong boundary indeed, even in small, isolated communities, and that we cannot assume that inter-ethnic contact will lead to assimilation.

The classic study on this was done by Labov who looked at people's orientation towards the local or wider community. 
### Region

AAVE is not homogeneous although this is a relatively under researched area.
### Voicing Multiple Identities

Language can index an individual's multiple identity, such as Korean-American use of AAVE to present as more masculine.
### Majority Ethnic Groups

Markers of whiteness are difficult to find because they are so normalised. They can also easily be confounded with ideas of social class or education. Research has identified using 'superstandard' forms as a marker of whiteness, e.g. "To whom am I speaking?"

Another relatively undocumented area is how:

>members of the dominant ethnic group who grow up in predominantly minority ethnic communities will usually acquire the local variety spoken by their peers

### Language Form and Language Function
#### Language Form: Sound Change

The traditional view is that members of minority groups do not generally participate in broader sound changes that are affecting the vernacular language of the majority populations. Labov was an early proponent of this view. This had been challenged by more recent studies.

It is important not to overgeneralise however:

> Even in a case where a majority of the ethnic community clearly is not participating in a sound change, some individuals may nonetheless be taking part. 
#### Language Function

Discourse patterns may also differ between ethnic groups e.g. with difference in turn-taking or the tolerance of silence.
### Future Directions

There is more work that remains to be done is areas such as language change in minority groups, identifying features of specific ethnolects, studying how ethnicity interacts with other features.
