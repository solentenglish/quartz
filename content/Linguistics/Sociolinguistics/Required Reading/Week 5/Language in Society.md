### Introduction

It is worthwhile tackling the deficit model of minority languages. Speakers of Spanish and African American dialects have been told that the way they speak is less expressive than standard forms. 
### Historical and contemporary colonial co-naturalizations of race and language**​

Race was at the centre of the European colonial project. Indiginous languages were devalued:

> In short, from the onset of European colonization, indigenous populations were stripped of their humanity at least in part through representations of their languages in animalistic terms that suggested they were incapable of expressing ideas that European colonizers thought were integral to becoming a full human being. 

Post-enlightenment, views differed on local languages. Some viewed them as worth preserving and evidence of pre-diluvian innocence whereas other felt they were impractical and an impediment to effective governance. Europeans learnt local languages in order to spread colonial epistemology.

>  Even when colonized subjects complied with the imposition of European languages, they continued to be positioned as racial Others who would never be fully European—and, by extension, fully human.

Colonial languages are still given a favoured position in the post-colonial era, often being seen as more technical or scientific.

Bi-lingual speakers of Spanish are discriminated against and considered:

> in need of linguistic remediation to provide them with access to the so-called ‘academic language’ required for complex thinking processes and successful engagement in the global economy 
### Perceptions of racial and linguistic difference

White bilingualism and minority bilingualism are viewed differently. One is seen as an achievement and something to be proud of whereas the other is seen a limiting and evidence of language deficit.
