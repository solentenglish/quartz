### Introduction

There are around 7000 languages in the world.

> The global distribution of languages across geographical space is uneven. 

The greatest variety of languages is centred on the equator because year round harvests enables groups to remain isolated from those around them.

Traditional patterns of multilingualism was disrupted by colonialism. Previous people knew the languages of the groups around them. Now, it is typically the dominant colonial language plus their mother tongue.

Increased mobility in recent years has also change intra-country multilingualism, with many languages now commonly spoken in one country.
### Individual multilingualism

There is debate around what constitutes multilingualism. Multilingualism can be varied in extent, e.g. technically even if you know one phrase in another language then you are multi-lingual. Also there are a variety of skills and systems which an individual might have varying proficiency in, e.g. speaking versus reading.
#### Definitions and examples

> Inter-Scandinavian communication is an example of what has been called “receptive multilingualism with productive monolingualism”
#### Becoming multilingual

Becoming truly multilingual is rare for adults once the have reached a certain age, especially with regard to phonology. Some adults are able to gain near native like mastery of a language however:

>  This suggests that the window period is relative rather than absolute, and success of acquisition also depends on the relationship/similarity of the languages involved, an individual’s motivation and the frequency of use of the second language.

There are a number of ways multilingualism can take place (see text for full list). One of the key characteristics is whether individuals will continue to have access to both languages once they have left the parental home.
#### Consequences of individual multilingualism

Codeswitching is often the norm in multilingual societies. In fact: 

> In some multilingual communities, high-frequency language alternation can become conventionalised, leading to the formation of a relatively stable mixed language variety for in-group communication

Attitudes to codeswitching can be ambiguous. On the one hand the standard form of the language is more prestigious. On the other hand, the ingroup functionality of codeswitching offers its own covert prestige.

In terms of language choice:

> Multilingual speakers often choose between languages depending on the situation and language competency of the interlocutor, as well as to mark identities or group affiliations, to negotiate social roles and status, and to establish interpersonal solidarity or distance.

Language shift is a common phenomenon in which subsequent generations of migrants move away from their original language and pick up that of the dominant variety. The rate that this occurs can vary between migrant groups and depends on factors such as initial language ability, marriage patterns, group hegemony etc.
### Societal multilingualism

Societies can be multilingual at the societal level and not the individual level, e.g. there can be different groups of monolingual speakers. Switzerland and Australia are countries that are often said to have a monolingual mindset.

Domains are a key idea when considering societal multilingualism. There are some situations in which one language is favoured over another. Examples of domains are: the home environment, work, school, peer group, neighbourhood, etc.

Some domains can be multi linguistic in themselves. This occurs when a country in multi linguistic at both the society and the individual level.

Diglossia refers to a stable system in which two or more languages exist in a region. This can either be broad or narrow. In a narrow sense, it means that there are clearly defined domains for each language and that this is imposed by an external authority. More common, is a broader definition in which certain domains favour but a not limited to a particular language.

Multilingualism is a separate process than that of adopting loan words. Loan words are often reworked and take on syntactical and phonological features of the L1.

There has been recent study of different countries linguistic environment, focusing on artefacts such as road sign and advertising.

Measures to promote languages other than the prestige language are often met with resistance. Speakers of non-colonial languages may which to benefit from learning the colonial language, e.g. to access education or work overseas.

Policies connected with multilingualism often play a key role in efforts to promote multiculturalism.
