## 1. Coming to terms

This chapter gives an overview of the key terms, notably sociolinguistics, language, language family and dialect.

Sociolinguistics is defined as:

> *the main focus of sociolinguistics is on language variation in different social context*

Dialects are variations of a language in 3 dimensions, pronunciation, grammar and vocabulary. It is stated that people are generally more tolerant of variations in vocabulary than in the other two areas.

All dialects are considered equal with none better or more valid than any other.

> It is logical to assume that every group of speakers has a communication system that works for them with the precision called for in their social circumstances and without ambiguity.

The definition of a dialect is problematic however as dialects of the same language may not be mutually comprehensible to one another.

One definition that has been given for a language is:

>  a language is a dialect that has an army and navy

Language families are groups of related languages. They were first studied in the 19th century when similarities were noted between Greek, Latin and Sanskrit. There is again little agreement upon what constitutes a language family and researchers are reliant upon other areas of research such as archaeology and genetics.
## 2. Variation and change

This chapter considers how language changes over time and not only geographically. Samples of writing from different types of English are shown and discussed. He discusses how there is a push and pull between divergence (group identity, isolation) and uniformity (comprehensibility, internationalism).

**Old English**: The first known version of English, spoken from the 5th century AD to 1066. It was influenced by Germanic tribes. 

- **Middle English**: Spoken from the 12th century to the late 15th century. It emerged after the Norman Conquest of 1066. 

- **Early Modern English**: Spoken from the 16th century. 

- **Late Modern English**: The version of English spoken today. 

> For tracking grammatical and lexical changes over time, there are written and printed texts (not always enough of them, of course).

Pronunciation changes are harder to track. Linguistics are reliant upon things such as how original texts scans, relationships between neighbouring languages and misspellings in texts.

Linguists use a variety of methods from the completion of surveys to participant investigations. It needs to be remembered that people change their language when they are conscious of being observed.

Labov's work on socio-economic status is considered, including his study of rhoticity in New York.

Studies have found differences in language use between genders.

Individuals are able to change the way that they speak, code-switching, to make imitate the language of other social classes. At times, this can mean that the language of lower status individuals can be a more exaggerated form of their target language. High status speakers can speak lower status forms in order to appear forceful and dynamic.

The way people interact with loan words can be illuminating. Men can be reluctant to say foreign words in a foreign manner for fear of sounding effeminate. Many loan words are adapted to sound more native. One reason for this is a desire not to sound pretentious.

A summary of the chapter is that:

> the underlying cause of sociolinguistic differences ... is the human instinct to establish and maintain social identity
## 3. Perceptions of language

The way language is perceived is based upon a person's pre-existing attitude which can be either positive or negative.

Dominant languages are generally seen positively.

> A fairly consistent finding across many contexts is that speakers of standard dialects or of socially prestigious varieties are perceived in broadly favorable ways

Why are some languages seen negatively / positively? There are three possible answers, some language are intrinsically better, they sound more attractive or it's due to prejudice.

> all aesthetic judgments rest upon “imposed norms”

Studies have shown that people without prior knowledge cannot identify the most 'beautiful' dialects of a language.

Traditionally maligned dialects such as ebonics have been shown to be as grammatically complex as other dialects.

> no dialects are substandard, but some are nonstandard.

The only remaining answer is prejudice.

> The variety itself is a trigger or stimulus that evokes attitudes(or prejudices or stereotypes) about the community to which the speaker is thought to belong. converge with or diverge from those of others.-

In fact, individuals from lower status speech communities will imitate the language of those in higher status groups. Often using language characteristic of these groups more frequently than pre-existing members do. If imitation brings positive benefits, then do lower status dialects continue to exists. Because, speakers who adopt other speech patterns run the risk of failing between two stools.

People are able to identify dialects that have negative associations but it is not known what it is that exactly triggers the prejudice, i.e. whether it is intonation, vocabulary etc.

There has also been close study of conversation in the field of discourse analysis / conversation analysis. This has been much criticised as it tends to create voluminous treatise with seemingly little practical application.

> initiation and termination of interchanges, the rules and practices governing turn-taking, interruptions and interjections, the appropriate sequencing of argument, the perils of miscommunication and their rectification, and the appropriateness of topics and styles of discussion.

## 4. Protecting language

There is a differences in approach between descriptivists and and prescriptivists.

> ... linguistic prescriptivism reflects the idea that language standards must be maintained and that unwanted influences are to be resisted.

>Contemporary manifestations include francophone attempts to keep English at bay,worries about language “decline,” complaints about the rising tide of slang, and concerns for “correctness.”

Sooner or later there is a recognition of the futility of language prescriptivism.

Prescriptivism is now such an anathema that linguistics might be thought to have abandoned a useful role.

Language planning has a long history. There are institutes in a large number of countries. Anglophone countries generally take a different approach; here the emphasis is on dictionaries.

There is a role for prescriptivism in the planning of a language, e.g. which dialect to select when formalising spelling etc.

> On the one hand, prescriptivism and purism based upon narrow and often unfair conceptions of social inclusion and exclusion; on the other, justifiable planning in behalf of broadly desirable normalization. The tension here is a permanent one.

There is also mention of language ecology. Which in the writer's opinion, is a field that has become preoccupied with language loss and the preservation of dying languages. Whilst diversity is generally a good things, there should be a recognition that the language landscape is dynamic and liable to change.

There is a tension between literacy and language preservation in that written languages tend to 'push oral ones aside'. This can hardly be an argument for refusing access to literacy though. Similarly education privileges written forms with the added affect of 'imposing' Western values.

It is questionable whether language rights exist beyond the level of an assertion.
